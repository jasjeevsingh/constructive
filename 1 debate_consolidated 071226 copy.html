Part 2

Debate is a search, not a fight

Three values define how we debate. They're also the basis for how you'll be judged.

1
Charitable Interpretation
Engage with what they meant, not the weakest version of what they said
If the other team's argument wasn't perfectly worded, assume the strongest possible version — then respond to that. A debate where both sides attack the weakest version of each other's argument teaches nothing. Engaging the strongest version forces everyone to think harder — including you.
Gurmat Connection
Guru Nanak Sahib traveled across the known world — through Hindu, Muslim, and other traditions — not to win arguments, but to understand and to be understood. The Udasis were not debates. They were searches. That spirit is what we are practicing here.
In the round
Before you refute, paraphrase — accurately enough that they'd nod.Setting up a weaker version to knock down is called a Straw Man. Judges catch it.
Concede a minor point when the other side earns it. Then explain why your side still wins.That's strategy, not weakness.
2
Reasoning Over Evidence
A clear chain of logic beats a pile of data with no logic
What matters is why something is true — the logical chain connecting your claim to reality. Evidence supports reasoning. It doesn't replace it. A debater who can explain why something follows is always more persuasive than one who can only say that it does.
Gurmat Connection
Bhai Gurdas writes that the Gurmukh speaks without pride — not to perform knowledge, but to share understanding. In Gurmat, wisdom that is lived and reasoned carries more weight than wisdom that is merely cited.
In the round
Build the "because" before you reach for a statistic. If the logic doesn't hold, the stat won't save it.
When you cite a source, say what it proves. Don't assume it speaks for itself.A study you can't explain is an argument you haven't finished building.
3
Listening Is Part of the Round
The best debaters are the best listeners
If you spend your opponent's speech thinking about what you'll say next, you'll miss what they actually argued. Listening is tactical. The debater who hears what was actually said — not what they expected — is the one who can respond to it.
Gurmat Connection
Sangat — the practice of collective learning through shared presence — is one of the foundational principles of Sikhi. You do not grow in isolation. You grow in community. That begins with actually hearing the person in front of you.
In the round
Take notes during every speech. The shift you miss in minute four is what the other team closes with.
When you're not speaking, stay visibly engaged — posture, stillness, eye contact.Judges watch the whole team, not just who's at the podium.
From Sikh History
When Mughal Emperor Babar invaded the Punjab, Guru Nanak Sahib witnessed it firsthand. Everyone around him was asking: who won? The Guru asked the deeper question: where were the millions of spiritual leaders when the people needed them — and what does it mean when those who claim moral authority disappear at the moment of real consequence? That question, from the Babarvani composed in the early 1500s, is still one of the sharpest debate motions ever posed. The question beneath the question is not a debate technique. It is a Sikh instinct.

Part 3

Five roles,
one team

Five roles. Each one has a different job. Select a role to see exactly what you're responsible for.

Introducer
Opens the round · Most prepared speech
You speak first. Your job is to frame the debate before the other side has said a word. Everything you say is written in advance — this is the most prepared role on the team.
  • Open with a hook — a scenario, a question, or a single striking claim
  • Define the key terms your team has agreed on
  • Preview your team's main arguments so judges know what's coming
  • You can raise POIs during other speeches. Your own speech has no open window — no one can interrupt you
Good fit if you have limited debate experience. You know exactly what you're saying before you stand up.
1st Speaker
Builds the first argument
Your job is one argument, built completely. Like the Introducer, this speech is pre-written — you know exactly what you're saying before you stand up. You speak before the other team has responded to anything, so go deep, not broad.
  • Build your team's strongest argument using ARESR
  • One argument done thoroughly beats three done quickly
  • No rebuttal — your teammates handle the response
  • First and last minute of your speech are protected — open window in between
Good fit if you think in clear steps and like having a focused, well-defined job.
2nd Speaker
Adds a new argument · Starts to respond
By the time you speak, the debate has started. You bring a new argument and begin pushing back on the other side.
  • Deliver a new team argument — don't repeat the 1st Speaker
  • Pick the other team's strongest point and directly counter it
  • Take notes during every speech before yours — your job starts with listening
  • First and last minute of your speech are protected — open window in between
Good fit if you're a strong listener and can handle both building and responding in the same speech.
1st Rebuttalist
Attacks the other side's case · Defends your own
Pure response. No new team arguments. Your job is to take apart the two or three most important things the other team said.
  • Pick their strongest arguments — not every point, just the ones that matter most
  • For each: name it, paraphrase it fairly, then show where it fails
  • Take notes throughout the round — the arguments you need to rebut are scattered across multiple speeches
  • Depth beats breadth — but explain why those points are the most significant ones in the debate
  • First and last minute of your speech are protected — open window in between
Good fit if you're a sharp listener who enjoys finding the flaw in a logical chain.
2nd Rebuttalist
Closes the case · Hardest role
You're the last team speaker. You've watched the whole debate unfold. Your job is to respond to what actually happened — and then close your team's case. This is the most demanding role on the team.
  • Respond to arguments your team hasn't fully addressed yet
  • Name the central clashes — the key points where the teams disagreed — and explain why your side won them
  • Close your team's case: synthesize the debate, don't just repeat arguments
  • Your speech is fully protected — no open window, no POIs
Good fit if you've been paying close attention all round, can see the big picture, and are comfortable thinking on your feet.
Part 4

How the round flows

Proposition and Opposition alternate. The Introducer opens for each team. The 2nd Rebuttalist speaks last — responding to the full debate and closing the team's case. Opposition closes before Proposition, giving Proposition the final word.
