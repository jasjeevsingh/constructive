Foundational Skill

How to read
a debate motion

Every debate starts with a motion — a statement your team either supports (Proposition) or challenges (Opposition). Before you build a single argument, run the motion through five moves.

Motion
"This House believes that homework should be banned."
1 · Core claim
Assigning homework causes more harm than good and schools should stop requiring it.
2 · Key words to define
"Homework" — all assigned work, or just busywork? "Banned" — eliminated, or capped? For which students? What age, what grade, what context? Define your terms before the other side defines them for you.
3 · Three strong arguments for this side
Homework increases stress and cuts into sleep, exercise, and family time. It widens the gap between students who have support at home and those who don't. And it's assigned relentlessly despite little evidence it improves learning.

Introduce all three up front: "Homework should be banned because, first, it increases student stress; second, it deepens inequality; and third, it lacks any proven benefit." That's signposting — it tells the room where you're going before you go there.
4 · What the other side will say
Homework builds discipline, reinforces classroom learning, and prepares students for the independent demands of high school and college.
Deeper question
This motion looks like a question about schoolwork — but it's really about what school is for. Is it to maximize learning? Build character? Prepare kids for a world that demands output regardless of how they feel? Whoever frames that question first usually controls the debate.
5 · World Visualization
Imagine the world where the motion is true. What does it actually look like — and how does it compare to the status quo?
Proposition world
No homework. Students leave school at 3pm with the afternoon free. They sleep longer, play sports, spend time with family. Teachers adjust — more learning happens in class. Students come in rested and ready.
Opposition world
No homework. Students fall behind on practice and reading. Material from class fades without reinforcement. By the time they reach high school, they've never had to manage their own study time — and it shows.
Why this matters
World visualization is how you build your Significance and frame your opening. It's especially powerful for the Introducer — it lets you paint a picture before you've presented a single piece of evidence. It also forces you to think through the real consequences of winning, not just the logic of your argument.

Work through all five moves before your first team prep call. When you can answer them, you're ready to build.

From Sikh History

When Mughal Emperor Babar invaded the Punjab, Guru Nanak Sahib witnessed it firsthand. Everyone around him was asking: who won? The Guru asked the deeper question: where were the millions of spiritual leaders when the people needed them — and what does it mean when those who claim moral authority disappear at the moment of real consequence? That question, from the Babarvani composed in the early 1500s, is still one of the sharpest debate motions ever posed. Finding the question beneath the question is not a debate technique. It is a Sikh instinct.

How to build
an argument

Every strong argument is built from five domains — ARESR. You don't always deliver all five in one breath, but every complete argument needs all five. Learn each one, then watch how they stack on a single motion throughout this section.

A Assertion R Reasoning E Evidence S Significance R Refutation POI Point of Info
A
Assertion
Your position, stated plainly
What it is
A clear, direct claim that takes a side. Not a question, not a fact — a position. The other side should be able to disagree with it.
Open with "I believe that..." or skip straight to the point. What matters is that your claim is specific and contestable. Push for specificity — vague claims are hard to defend and easy for the other side to sidestep.
✗ Broad — not specific enough
"Homework is bad for students." This is a theme, not a claim. It doesn't say why or what the impact is — nothing for the other side to grab onto.
✓ Specific and contestable
"Homework takes up too much student free time." Specific. Contestable. Implies a real impact. The other side has something to fight.

Reasoning
The "because" — why your claim is true
What it is
The logic that connects your assertion to reality. Don't repeat the claim — explain the chain of cause and effect that makes it true.
One-sentence test: Can you complete "[Assertion] because [Reasoning]" and have it make sense? If yes, you're on track.
Aristotle's three appeals — a useful lens, not a checklist
These categories can help you check whether your reasoning is grounded. But don't chase all three — a single, clear logical chain is better than trying to hit every appeal. Logos is almost always what wins in a debate round.

E
Evidence
Proof that your reasoning isn't just opinion
What it is
A specific fact, study, statistic, or real-world example that backs up your reasoning. Evidence shows the judges your logic is grounded in something real. You'll receive a packet of articles before the retreat — that's your foundation. You can also bring in your own research if you want to go further.
Data / Stats
Numbers from a study or report
Expert Opinion
What researchers or specialists have found
Anecdote
A real story that illustrates the point
Historical Example
Something that already happened in the world
Don't just drop a fact. Frame it.
"According to [source], [finding] — which shows that [connection to your reasoning]."
Evidence-specific pitfalls
Appeal to Authority: Don't cite a source just because it sounds impressive. "Harvard researchers say..." means nothing if you can't explain what they found and why it applies. Good evidence is specific, not just credentialed.

Generalization: Make sure your evidence actually applies to the situation you're arguing about. A study on college students may not apply to middle schoolers. A statistic about one country may not apply to another.

Significance
Coming next →
S
Significance
The "so what" — who's affected and how much
What it is
Where you zoom out and explain the real-world impact. Two questions to answer: Who is affected, and how many? And: What actually changes if your side is right?
A strong Significance makes one or more clear comparisons.

Three tools that help you build those comparisons:

Magnitude — How many people are affected? The bigger the scale, the stronger the case.

Probability — How likely is this outcome? Certain harms matter more than speculative ones.

Timeframe — When does this happen? Immediate impacts often outweigh distant ones.

Use the ones that apply. Don't force all three into every argument — only include a comparison if it actually sharpens your point.
Roles: 2nd Rebuttalist 2nd Speaker Rebuttalists
See it in action — the full argument
Assertion
Homework takes up too much student free time.
Reasoning
When students spend hours on homework every night, they lose time for sleep, exercise, and things that shape who they become.
Evidence
Stanford researchers found more than three hours of nightly homework increased stress and health problems — even among motivated students who were working hard.
Significance
This isn't one school's problem — it's happening to millions of students right now. The ones hit hardest are often the ones with the least support at home: kids whose parents work late, who don't have a quiet place to study, who are already stretched. Piling on homework doesn't raise standards. It widens the gap between kids who can absorb the pressure and kids who can't.
R
Refutation
Taking apart the other side's argument
What it is
Refutation isn't just disagreeing — and it isn't about proving the other side is wrong. It's about showing why your point matters more. Good refutation is offensive, not defensive: you're not defending your position, you're attacking theirs. The goal is to show that even if they have a valid point, yours has greater significance.
The four-step structure
They say
Summarize in 3–7 words
Shows you listened. Keeps it clean.
But
State your counter directly
Plants your flag before the explanation.
Because
Your reasoning and evidence
This is where you do the work.
Therefore
Compare both arguments
Helps the room see who won the point.
Ways to win a refutation
Their logic has a flaw — name the fallacy if you can.
Their evidence is outdated, weak, or from a biased source.
Their point actually supports your side if you think it through.
Your argument has greater significance — more people, higher stakes.
The "Even if" move: You don't always need to prove the other side is wrong. You can acknowledge their point and still win the refutation.

"Even if homework does build some discipline, the Stanford evidence shows it causes more harm than that benefit is worth. Their gain doesn't outweigh our loss."

This shows you're engaging with their argument seriously — which is also what charitable interpretation looks like in practice.
Tactic
Point of Information (POI)
During any speech, you can rise and say "Point of information." If the speaker accepts, you have 15 seconds to ask a question that challenges what they just said. A well-timed POI can shake the other side mid-argument. Have your question ready before you stand.
Roles: 2nd Speaker 1st Rebuttalist 2nd Rebuttalist Any speaker (POI)
See it in action — Opposition argues: "Homework builds discipline."
They say
Homework builds discipline.
But
Discipline built through exhaustion isn't real discipline — it's compliance.
Because
Students do their best learning when they're rested and motivated, not grinding through assignments at 11pm. If the habit being built is "push through no matter how you feel," that's a recipe for burnout — not a skill. The Stanford evidence shows heavy homework increases stress and reduces learning quality, which means the reinforcement they're describing isn't even happening.
Therefore
The opposition's strongest argument — that homework builds good habits — falls apart under the evidence. Their case doesn't just fail. It supports banning the kind of homework that produces these outcomes.
POI
Point of Information
The live interruption — used by all speakers
What it is
A POI is a brief interruption during an opponent's speech. You stand, say "Point of information," and if the speaker accepts, you have 15 seconds to challenge what they just said. POIs are one of the only moments in the debate where the two sides are directly in conversation — and judges notice how you handle them on both sides of the exchange.
The rules
POIs can only be offered during the open window — after the first minute and before the last minute of any open speech. The open window applies to: 1st Speaker, 2nd Speaker, and 1st Rebuttalist speeches. The 2nd Rebuttalist's speeches are fully protected — no POIs, on either side.

The speaker holding the floor decides whether to accept. They are not required to take every POI — but they should accept at least one or two per speech. Judges mark down speakers who accept none, and also mark down speakers who never bother to offer any.
How to offer a good POI
Ask it as a question, not a statement. "Doesn't your evidence only apply to high school students, not middle schoolers?" forces the speaker to engage. A flat statement they can ignore without answering.

Target a point that sets up your refutation. A POI on a weak claim wastes your 15 seconds on something the room already doubts. A POI on a strong claim that you can directly counter shows you're engaging the debate at its core — and it opens the door for your next speech.

Have your counter ready before you stand. If they accept, you have 15 seconds. Know exactly what you're going to say.

Keep it short and sharp. Long POIs ramble and lose the room. One clear challenge is more effective than a mini-speech.
How to respond when you accept
You don't have to fully answer the question — you have to control the exchange. Three options:

Answer and move on. If their point is weak, dispatch it in one sentence and return to yours.

Flip it. Show how their question actually supports your side. "Exactly — and that's why we argue…"

Defer and return. "I'll address that point directly in my next argument." Only works if you actually do.
Roles: All speakers during open window Not available: 2nd Rebuttalist speeches · First and last minute of any open speech
See it in action — Offering a POI
Stand + offer
"Point of information."
If accepted
"Your Stanford study covered high schoolers with more than three hours of homework. Doesn't that mean it says nothing about students who have less?"
Speaker responds
"The Stanford study is one piece of evidence among several we're citing. Studies across age groups and homework loads consistently show the same pattern: when homework exceeds what students can reasonably absorb, stress rises and learning drops. The issue isn't the specific number — it's that the load routinely exceeds what's beneficial. Our argument doesn't depend on a single data point."
See it in action — Declining a POI
Stand + offer
"Point of information."
Speaker declines
"Not now, thank you." — and continues their speech. The POI-offerer sits down. They can try again later in the open window.
Judges are watching both sides of the exchange. Offering sharp POIs shows you're tracking the debate. Handling them well when you accept shows you can think on your feet. Both count toward your score.
Give your teammates space. If one person on your team is offering POI after POI, other speakers miss their chance to be heard by the judges. Spread the offers across your team — and if a teammate stands first, sit down.
